A neural net that reads stock charts

It looks at a picture of a stock's last 20 trading days, the way a chartist would, and estimates the odds that the stock rises over the next month. Buying the top 10% of its picks and shorting the bottom 10% earned about {{ls_ann}}% a year for 26 years, before trading costs.

How it works

Draw the chart

Every day, each of about 3,000 Russell 3000 stocks becomes a 64 × 60 pixel black-and-white image: daily price bars, a 20-day moving average and volume.

Let the network read it

A convolutional network, the kind used for image recognition, outputs the probability of a positive 20-day return. Five copies are averaged.

Trade the extremes

Buy the highest-ranked 10% and short the lowest 10%, equal weight, held a month. The model is retrained each year only on the past.

This replicates Jiang, Kelly & Xiu, "(Re-)Imag(in)ing Price Trends" (Journal of Finance, 2023). Every year shown is out of sample: it is scored by a model that never saw that year.

What the network actually sees

Where it works best

Sharpe ratio of the long-short book. Sector-neutral means it buys and shorts equal amounts within each sector, which removes industry bets and lifts the Sharpe in every slice. Small caps are the sweet spot.

The fine print

  • All returns are gross: no trading costs, borrow fees or slippage. The strategy turns over heavily every month, so real-world returns would be meaningfully lower, especially in small caps.
  • It has faded. The Sharpe ratio was {{p1_sharpe}} over 2000–2011 and {{p2_sharpe}} over 2012–2026. The last three years returned {{y3_ann}}% a year, and the last twelve months {{y1_ann}}%.
  • Much of the edge overlaps well-known price effects, like short-term reversal and the tendency of lottery-like stocks to disappoint. Controlling for those, the leftover signal was strong before 2012 and roughly zero since.
  • It works far better in small caps (Sharpe {{small_sharpe}}) than large caps ({{large_sharpe}}).
  • Results use 20 staggered monthly portfolios, so they don't depend on a lucky rebalance date, and they include stocks that later went bankrupt or were delisted.
